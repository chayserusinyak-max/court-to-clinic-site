The Overlooked Foundation: Ankle Training in Youth Sports

Written by a 10th-grade student passionate about sports medicine and recovery.

In youth sports, training programs often emphasize speed ladders, vertical jump tests, and heavy weight-room sessions. While those drills build power, they sometimes ignore the smaller structures that keep athletes upright—especially the ankles. Research shows that ankle injuries account for nearly 30% of injuries in court and field sports, yet ankle strength and balance rarely get their own slot on the training schedule.

Why Young Athletes Need Strong Ankles

The ankle is a complex joint made up of bones, ligaments, and tendons that stabilize every cut and landing. When those tissues are weak or stiff, the rest of the body pays the price. Instability at the ankle can travel up the kinetic chain, stressing knees and hips. For growing athletes, repeated ankle sprains can even change how they move, making them cautious and less explosive.

Common Mistakes in Practice

Too often, warm-ups skip mobility work in favor of quick sprints. Athletes are also asked to compete year-round, so they rarely get time for a structured strength phase. Another problem: many youth programs copy professional workouts that assume athletes already have strong ankles. Without foundational work, advanced plyometrics only increase the risk of rolling an ankle.

Building a Better Routine

Every practice can include a three-part ankle routine in less than ten minutes:

  1. Mobility: Ankle circles, calf stretches against a wall, and kneeling dorsiflexion drills keep the joint moving through a full range.
  2. Strength: Single-leg calf raises, band-resisted eversion and inversion, and slow heel-to-toe walks build durable tendons and muscles.
  3. Balance: Standing on one leg with eyes closed or on a foam pad trains proprioception—the body’s ability to know where it is in space.

Integrating Ankles into Team Culture

Coaches can set the tone by demonstrating ankle drills and tracking progress. Athletic trainers and parents can support by encouraging athletes to perform the routine at home on rest days. Celebrate improvements, such as landing a single-leg hop more quietly or balancing for longer without wobbling.

The Payoff

When youth athletes prioritize ankle strength, they cut and jump with more confidence. Injuries decrease, and athletes spend more time practicing instead of rehabbing. Strong ankles are the foundation for explosive movement—and the best time to build that foundation is now.
